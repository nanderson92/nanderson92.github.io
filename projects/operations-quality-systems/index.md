I managed a McDonald’s drive-through that served 110 cars per hour at peak. Here’s what that taught me about bottlenecks, quality loops, and keeping a system running when everything is breaking.

## What I learned

| Operations experience | Manufacturing-relevant behavior |
|---|---|
| Peak rush line balancing | Recognizing bottlenecks while the system is moving |
| Food safety / CCP logs | Quality discipline and documentation under time pressure |
| Preventive maintenance routines | Uptime, reliability, and small-fix-before-big-failure mindset |
| Crew training | Standard work and execution consistency |

## The pivot

Early on, I tried to solve rushes by working faster myself. That failed because the bottleneck just moved. The better answer was changing where people stood, what they checked, and when they reset the system before it broke.

## Roles represented

<div class="matrix role-grid"><div class="matrix-card"><h3>McDonald’s Manager</h3><p>I learned that speed was not heroic effort; it was station layout, handoff timing, and resetting the line before the bottleneck moved.</p></div><div class="matrix-card"><h3>Chick-fil-A Lead / Crew Trainer</h3><p>I trained for repeatability: the same standard had to survive different people, rush periods, and constant interruptions.</p></div><div class="matrix-card"><h3>Flapdoodles Manager</h3><p>I learned how small teams keep equipment, inventory, and service moving when there is almost no slack in the system.</p></div></div>

## What I’d do next

In a hardware startup, I would apply the same operating instinct to pilot lines: define the bottleneck, make the handoff visible, write the simplest standard work that actually gets followed, and track the failure mode that burns the most time.
