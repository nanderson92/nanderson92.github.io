<section class="technical-credibility-strip" aria-label="Technical summary">
 <p class="system-label small">TECHNICAL DIRECTION</p>
 <h2>I use chemical engineering to turn fragile lab behavior into repeatable process windows.</h2>
 <p>At Georgia Tech, my current work in the Filler Lab studies a manufacturing concept where semiconductor devices could be produced and assembled in a smaller, faster, less facility-intensive way than conventional fabrication. The work has pushed me to define boundary conditions, measurable outputs, and decision rules instead of only describing what I observed.</p>
 <p>That same process-development lens connects my Mayo Clinic microencapsulation work, printed-interconnect reliability testing, embedded controls projects, and high-throughput operations experience.</p>
</section>

 <article class="background-copy">
  <p class="system-label small">INCHEON → MINNESOTA → ATLANTA</p>
  <p>I was adopted from Incheon at seven months old and grew up in small-town Minnesota: too Korean for some rooms, not Korean enough for others. That in-between feeling made me notice systems early: who they include, who they leave out, and what technology has to become before it actually reaches people.</p>
  <p>The future often appears first as a fragile lab result. The harder question is what it takes to make that result reliable enough to leave the lab. That is why chemical engineering became the right language for me: transport, materials, controls, manufacturing, and scale-up as the bridge from discovery to infrastructure.</p>
 </article>
</div>
