 <article>
 <h2>Why process engineering</h2>
 <p>I study process development for advanced manufacturing. At Filler Lab · Georgia Tech, micromodular printed-electronics work forced me to define inputs, <abbr title="Critical-to-Quality">CTQ</abbr> metrics, screens, and failure modes instead of only describing what I observed.</p>
 <p>Earlier Mayo Clinic work focused on microfluidic encapsulation throughput and thermocycler automation for biological protocols.</p>
 </article>
 <article>
 <h2>Why systems work</h2>
 <p>The useful question is not only whether a result worked once. It is whether the variables, tools, and people around it can make the result happen again.</p>
 <p>I am most interested in the handoff from observation to measurement to control plan.</p>
 </article>
 <article>
 <h2>Why future infrastructure</h2>
 <p>The future I care about is physical: cleaner factories, better wet labs, accessible biotech, resilient energy systems, and advanced manufacturing that reaches more than privileged rooms.</p>
 </article>
</section>

<section class="access-feature tone-dark">
 <p class="system-label">Why access matters</p>
 <h2>Systems decide who gets to use the future.</h2>
 <p>Growing up adopted from Incheon into small-town Minnesota made me notice who systems are built for. That observation belongs here, after the technical frame, because it explains why manufacturability matters to me.</p>
</section>

<section id="qsr-mini-case" class="mcdonalds-mini-case pull-quote-block">
 <p class="system-label">Quick-Service Restaurant (QSR) operations mini-case</p>
 <p>Peak-hour operations made bottlenecks visible in minutes. Prep, sequencing, staffing, equipment uptime, and handoff timing behaved like a live control problem. One improvement cycle produced a +20% cars/hr increase during peak shifts, based on shift-level throughput tracking. That operator instinct now shows up in how I read wet-lab workflows and early manufacturing screens.</p>
</section>
