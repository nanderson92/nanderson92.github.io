<section class="background-depth reordered-background three-section-rhythm">
 <article>
  <h2>Why process engineering</h2>
  <p>I develop processes for advanced manufacturing. Filler Lab printed-electronics work and Mayo Clinic microfluidics both pushed me toward the same question: what inputs, failure modes, and measurements make a result repeatable?</p>
 </article>
 <article>
  <h2>Why systems work</h2>
  <p>The useful question is not only whether a result worked once. It is whether the variables, tools, materials, and people around it can make the result happen again under pressure.</p>
 </article>
 <article>
  <h2>Why future infrastructure</h2>
  <p>The future I care about is physical: cleaner factories, better wet labs, accessible biotech, resilient energy systems, and advanced manufacturing that reaches more than privileged rooms.</p>
 </article>
</section>

<section class="mcdonalds-mini-case pull-quote-block compact-operator-arc">
 <p class="system-label">Operator-to-process-engineer arc</p>
 <p>Visible failure is usually late evidence. Prep, sequencing, staffing, equipment uptime, and handoff timing often decide the outcome before the miss appears. That operator instinct now shows up in how I read wet-lab workflows and early manufacturing screens.</p>
</section>

<section class="access-feature tone-dark compact-access-feature">
 <p class="system-label">Why access matters</p>
 <h2>Systems decide who gets to participate in the future.</h2>
 <p>Growing up adopted from Incheon into small-town Minnesota taught me that access is not automatic. Some people inherit systems built around them. Others have to learn how those systems work from the outside.</p>
 <p>That is why I care about manufacturability. Discovery matters, but discovery alone does not change who gets access to new technology. The real question is whether a breakthrough can become affordable, reliable, scalable, and usable by people who were never in the room where it was invented.</p>
</section>
