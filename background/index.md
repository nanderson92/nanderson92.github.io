<p class="background-lede">I am a Georgia Tech chemical engineering student building process-development judgment in wet processing, printed electronics, biotech fabrication, and clean-technology deployment.</p>

<section class="background-technical-summary background-depth" aria-label="Technical summary">
  <article class="technical-summary-card">
    <p class="system-label small">TECHNICAL CENTER FIRST</p>
    <h2>Process development for technologies that need to leave the lab.</h2>
    <p>My strongest work sits where an interesting result becomes a repeatable process: defining boundary conditions, collecting the right signals, and turning observations into operating windows.</p>
  </article>
  <article>
    <h2>Filler Lab · Georgia Tech</h2>
    <p class="promoted-line"><strong>I study a manufacturing concept where semiconductor devices could be produced and assembled in a smaller, faster, less facility-intensive way than conventional fabrication.</strong></p>
    <p>That work forced me to define droplet-deposition boundary conditions, measurable outputs, and decision rules rather than only describe what I observed.</p>
  </article>
  <article>
    <h2>Mayo Clinic · microfluidic process development</h2>
    <p>My Mayo Clinic work showed me how scale-up exposes the gap between a promising bench demo and a process that can repeatedly make usable capsules under real constraints.</p>
  </article>
</section>

 <article class="background-copy">
 <p class="system-label small">INCHEON → MINNESOTA → ATLANTA</p>
 <p>
 I was adopted from Incheon at seven months old and grew up in small-town Minnesota — too Korean for some rooms, not Korean enough for others. That in-between feeling made me notice systems early: who they include, who they leave out, and what technology has to become before it actually reaches people.
 </p>
 <p>
 The future often appears first as a fragile lab result. The harder question is what it takes to make that result reliable enough to leave the lab. That is why chemical engineering became the right language for me: transport, materials, controls, manufacturing, and scale-up as the bridge from discovery to infrastructure.
 </p>
 </article>
</div>

    <h2>What “future built environment” means to me</h2>
    <p>To me, the future built environment means clean technology made physical. I care about the systems that make advanced manufacturing and low-carbon infrastructure visible in everyday life: factories, devices, energy systems, and biotech tools that people can actually use.</p>
  </article>
