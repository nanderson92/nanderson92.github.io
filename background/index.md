 <article class="background-copy">
 <p class="system-label small">INCHEON → MINNESOTA → ATLANTA</p>
 <p>
 I was adopted from Incheon at seven months old and grew up in small-town Minnesota — too Korean for some rooms, not Korean enough for others. That in-between feeling made me notice systems early: who they include, who they leave out, and what technology has to become before it actually reaches people.
 </p>
 <p>
 The future often appears first as a fragile lab result. The harder question is what it takes to make that result reliable enough to leave the lab. That is why chemical engineering became the right language for me: transport, materials, controls, manufacturing, and scale-up as the bridge from discovery to infrastructure.
 </p>
 </article>
</div>

  <article>
    <h2>At Georgia Tech</h2>
    <p>Georgia Tech gave that motivation a process-engineering direction. In my micromodular electronics work, I study a manufacturing concept where semiconductor devices could be produced and assembled in a smaller, faster, less facility-intensive way than conventional fabrication. The research forced me to define boundary conditions, measurable outputs, and decision rules rather than only describe what I observed.</p>
  </article>

  <article>
    <h2>What “future built environment” means to me</h2>
    <p>To me, the future built environment means a clean-technology future made physical: advanced materials, energy systems, semiconductor manufacturing, biotech tools, and clean infrastructure working at city and industrial scale. Impact happens when ideas become reliable, affordable, manufacturable, and visible in everyday infrastructure.</p>
  </article>
