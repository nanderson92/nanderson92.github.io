<p class="background-lede">I am a Georgia Tech chemical engineering student focused on process development: turning fragile lab results into repeatable, measurable systems.</p>

<section class="background-depth refined-background-depth">
  <article>
    <h2>Technical direction</h2>
    <p class="callout-line">In my micromodular electronics work at Georgia Tech, I study a manufacturing concept where semiconductor devices could be produced and assembled in a smaller, faster, less facility-intensive way than conventional fabrication.</p>
    <p>That work pushed me toward the questions I care about most: what variables control a process, what measurements prove it is repeatable, and what decision rule tells a team to accept, reject, or redesign a manufacturing route?</p>
  </article>
</section>

 <article class="background-copy">
 <p class="system-label small">INCHEON → MINNESOTA → ATLANTA</p>
 <p>I was adopted from Incheon at seven months old and grew up in small-town Minnesota — too Korean for some rooms, not Korean enough for others. That in-between feeling made me notice systems early: who they include, who they leave out, and what technology has to become before it actually reaches people.</p>
 <p>The future often appears first as a fragile lab result. The harder question is what it takes to make that result reliable enough to leave the lab. That is why chemical engineering became the right language for me: transport, materials, controls, manufacturing, and scale-up as the bridge from discovery to infrastructure.</p>
 </article>

<section class="background-depth refined-background-depth">
  <article>
    <h2>At Georgia Tech</h2>
    <p>Georgia Tech gave that motivation a process-engineering direction. My current work asks how substrate boundary conditions, droplet transport, and reliability metrics can become useful manufacturing decisions instead of isolated observations.</p>
    <p>Working knowledge of MBE deposition and SEM/XRD/XPS/Raman characterization from undergraduate semiconductor materials research also informs how I think about linking process conditions to material response.</p>
  </article>

  <article>
    <h2>What “future built environment” means to me</h2>
    <p>To me, the future built environment means clean technology made physical. I care about the systems that make advanced manufacturing and low-carbon infrastructure visible in everyday life: factories, devices, energy systems, and biotech tools that people can actually use.</p>
  </article>

  <article>
    <h2>Bridge to technical work</h2>
    <p>The projects on this site are that bridge in practice. Each one took a fragile lab result or live operating constraint — droplet behavior, resistance traces, capsule yield, thermal stability, or drive-through flow — and asked what it would take to repeat it reliably.</p>
